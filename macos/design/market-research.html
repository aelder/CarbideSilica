Marketplace Research

How the leading fields present a set of open offers, and what that means for the Marketplace page. Each example uses the open offers on the Verus chain, read from the local node.

The short answer: the data decides this one more than the literature does. The page today draws the offers as a market: a cloud of points by price and time. The offers are not a market in that sense. They are a catalogue of names, nearly all from one seller, at a few fixed prices. The fields that present catalogues well start with search and filters that show their counts, state the structure in words, and make the item itself the largest thing on the page.

What the offers are

  • It is a catalogue of names. Each offer but four is an identity name for sale, priced in VRSC. The four others are small currency offers.
  • One seller is the market. The offers come from a small number of addresses, and one address has nearly all of them. The offers of the other sellers are the only independent prices, and the page today hides them in the cloud.
  • Prices are steps, not a spread. Most offers sit at one of a few exact prices. A scatter plot puts hundreds of points on one spot.
  • Time tells little. No offer is recent, and no offer expires soon. The page today gives its whole horizontal axis to the time before expiry.
  • The names are the content. They are in more than ten scripts, and many are emoji. A person looks for a name, a kind of name, or a price.

What the leading fields do

Dynamic queries

Ahlberg and Shneiderman's FilmFinder and HomeFinder are the origin of the page today: items as points on two axes, with sliders that filter in less than a tenth of a second. Their rule is tight coupling: each control shows what it will do, and each result can start the next question. The form works when the two axes are what a person decides by, and when the points spread.

For the Marketplace: keep the immediate filters. The two axes of the plot are not what a person decides by here.

Scented widgets

Willett, Heer and Agrawala put a small chart inside each control: a histogram on a slider, a count beside an option. A person sees where the data is before the click. Baymard's tests of shop filters reach the same rule: show the number of results beside each option, and show which filters are applied.

For the Marketplace: each filter option carries its count and a bar. An empty option is shown as empty.

Name marketplaces

The markets for Ethereum names sort the names into categories that buyers think in: three digits, four letters, one emoji. OpenSea's name collection lists these as filters with their counts. A category is a set of filters with a name.

For the Marketplace: filter by the length of the name and by its kind: letters, digits, emoji, other scripts.

Domain marketplaces

Sites that sell domain names start with a search field. The filters are the budget, the length and the listing date. The results are a list sorted by price, and a list of matches can be exported. Operators of these sites say that search is the feature that decides whether the site works.

For the Marketplace: a search field over the names first, then filters, then a sortable list.

Exchanges

An exchange shows its open orders as a price ladder and as a depth chart: the total quantity available at or better than each price. A buyer reads it as "how much can I get for this much".

For the Marketplace: one curve of the number of names available at or below each price.

Ranking tables

Table Lens (Rao and Card) and LineUp (Gratzl and colleagues) put a bar inside each number cell of a table. The table stays exact and sortable, and the eye compares the bars. LineUp won the best paper award at InfoVis in 2013.

For the Marketplace: a bar under each price in the list.

Overplotting

Wilke's guide and the ggplot2 references agree: when many points have the same values, a scatter plot fails. The remedies are transparency, jitter, bins, or a count. For a few exact values, a bar for each value is the clearest.

For the Marketplace: prices as bars with counts, not as points.

Name safety

The Ethereum name standard (ENSIP-15) exists because names can hold look-alike letters, invisible characters and joined emoji. Its rules: show the normalized form, and refuse a name that cannot be read safely. Two names that look the same are two different registrations.

For the Marketplace: show the script of a name that is not in Latin letters, and its code points on request.

The same offers, three ways

The third way is not a chart. It is the catalogue below.

A working catalogue

These are the real offers. Each option shows its count for the current selection, with a bar. Select an option, and each other count changes at once. The line above the list states what is applied.

The seller is shown as the start and end of the address that controls the offered identity. The app has the full address and opens it.

The offers of the other sellers

These offers are the part of the page that a second seller would look for. Today they are points in the cloud.

What to build

  1. An answer line. The number of offers, the number of sellers, the share of the largest seller, the lowest price, the median price, and the age of the newest offer.
  2. The catalogue as the page. A search field over the names, filters with counts and bars (kind of name, length, price step, seller, listing period), a line that states the applied filters, and a list.
  3. The name as the largest thing in each row. One line for each offer: the name, the price with a bar, the listing date, the expiry, the seller. The links to the funding transaction, the identity and the address stay, in one compact group.
  4. The price steps as bars with counts, and one curve of the names available at or below each price, in place of the scatter plot.
  5. Sellers as a filter and as a short table. The offers of the smaller sellers get their own group, so that they are not lost.
  6. The script of a name that is not in Latin letters, and the code points of a name on request.
  7. Carbide's four sort orders stay (expiry, name, ask amount, listing age), with reverse, and the raw JSON.

One defect to correct

Each identity row today has a link named "Currency original". The offer has a field named original with the value 1, and the app reads it as a currency. It is not one.

What not to do

  • Keep the scatter plot as the main picture. Its two axes are the price, which has a few exact values, and the time before expiry, which does not matter here.
  • A treemap of sellers. One seller with nearly all offers is a sentence, not a picture.
  • A depth chart with two sides. There are no requests to buy on the chain now. The page must show that plainly when one appears.

The market can change. If more sellers arrive and prices spread, the price curve and the seller table show it without a change of design. That is the reason to prefer them to a form that fits only today's data.
