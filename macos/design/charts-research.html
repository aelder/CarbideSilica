Charts Research

How the leading fields present several measures of one system over time, and what that means for the Charts page. Each example is drawn from the same day of real Verus blocks, read from the local node.

The short answer: the fields that do this best agree on four things. Put the measures on one shared time axis with one cursor. Give each measure limits that come from its own history, so a person can tell a signal from noise. Never let the drawing lose a peak. State the answer in words before the chart. The Charts page today does none of the four.

What the day of data is like

These figures are from blocks, approximately hours.

  • The rate is steady and the gaps are not. Each hour has close to 60 blocks. The time between two blocks is anything from 0 seconds to more than 15 minutes. A chart of single gaps shows the noise. A chart of blocks in each hour shows the system.
  • The staked share is held, not left to chance. Over 100 blocks, coin flips would move the share by 5 points in one standard deviation. The chain moved it by half of that. The chain alternates staked and mined blocks, so a small move is already unusual.
  • Difficulty moves in a sawtooth. It changes with each block, and it covered a range of more than four times in the day. A trailing average of 100 blocks runs approximately 50 blocks behind the turns.
  • The fee pool is a leaking tank. In this day, each time the pool fell, it fell by 1.00%. It rises in steps when fees arrive. Carbide does not infer payout or inflow from these changes, and the app must not state them as facts.

What the leading fields do

Health-service reporting

NHS England's Making Data Count programme replaced red, amber and green ratings and two-point comparisons with statistical process control charts across the service. Each chart has the mean and two process limits that come from the measure's own variation. A point is coloured only when a rule finds a special cause: a point outside the limits, or a run of six or seven points on one side of the mean. A small icon states the result for people who do not read the chart.

For Charts: limits from the measure's own history, colour only for a signal, and one word that states the result.

Monitoring dashboards

Grafana stacks panels on one time range and offers a shared crosshair: the pointer in one panel draws the same moment in each panel, with each value. Marked events can be shown across all panels. Datadog draws a grey band for the range that the history predicts, and treats a point outside the band as an anomaly.

For Charts: one time axis, one cursor, the long waits marked through all panels, and a band for the usual range.

Market charts

Trading tools place price, volume and indicators in stacked panes that share the time scale. A crosshair crosses all panes, and a legend in each pane shows the value at the crosshair. The reader never looks away from the chart to find a number.

For Charts: the selected block's figures belong at the cursor, not below the fold.

Edward Tufte

Small multiples repeat one design so the eye compares the data and not the frames. A sparkline is a small, intense, word-sized graphic. His clinical examples add a grey band for the normal range, so a deviation is seen at once.

For Charts: the same frame for each measure, short panels, and a normal band in each.

Downsampling research

When a series has more points than the plot has pixels, the method of selection decides what the reader sees. Jugel and colleagues (M4) and the Ghent group (MinMax, LTTB) show that the minimum and maximum of each pixel column must be kept. Taking each nth point is the least faithful method: it loses peaks and it changes when the range moves.

For Charts: the fee pool is drawn from each nth block today. Draw the lowest and highest value of each column.

Perception research

Cleveland showed that people judge slopes best when the lines are near 45 degrees, so the shape of the frame changes what can be seen. Heer and Agrawala extended it to more than one scale. A line that uses one sixth of its frame is nearly flat and tells nothing. Bars must start at zero because their length is the value. Lines need not, because position is the value.

For Charts: fit the axis to the data for lines, and say so. Keep zero for bars.

Smoothing

A trailing average is late by approximately half of its window. A centred average is not late, but it uses later data, so it cannot be drawn up to the present. Charts of the past use a centred smoother. Live signals use a trailing one.

For Charts: keep Carbide's trailing MA100 and name it as trailing. Show the range of the raw values so the level is clear without the delay.

Overview and detail

Cockburn, Karlson and Bederson reviewed the ways to show detail and context together. The most common in time-series tools is a small overview of the full period with a brush that selects the range in the main plot. The reader always knows where the range is.

For Charts: an overview strip in place of blind Older and Newer steps.

Block explorers

mempool.space opens its mining page with the answer: the projected difficulty change, the blocks until it, and the last change. The long charts follow. Clark Moody's dashboard reports the pace of blocks against the target as a number.

For Charts: one line of answers above the plots: the rate, the change in each difficulty, the staked share, the fee pool.

All of them on one time axis

This is the form that Grafana, trading tools and Tufte's small multiples have in common. Move the pointer over the figure. One line crosses all panels, and the row above gives each value at that block. The faint vertical lines are the longest waits of the day. They show at once that a long wait, a fall in difficulty and a step in the fee pool are one event.

What to build

  1. One stack of panels on one time axis, in place of five charts behind a control: block rate, mining difficulty, staking difficulty, staked share, fee pool. Each panel is short and has the same frame.
  2. One shared cursor. The pointer or the arrow keys select a block. A line crosses all panels, and a readout row gives each value. The readout is above the panels, not below the fold.
  3. Limits from the measure's own history. The block rate gets a process control chart: the mean and two limits from the hourly counts. The staked share gets the limits that chance gives for 100 blocks. Colour is used only for a point that a rule marks.
  4. An answer line above the panels. For each measure: the value now, the change over the range, and one word, for example "in its usual range".
  5. No lost peaks. The fee pool and the dense ranges draw the lowest and highest value of each pixel column.
  6. Long waits as marks through all panels. The same rule as the dashboard pulse: a wait in the longest hundredth of the record.
  7. An overview strip of a longer period with the visible range marked. Older, Newer, the four spans and Go to stay.
  8. The waits of the range as a dot plot, in the same form as the dashboard pulse, in place of the bar for each gap.

What stays as Carbide defines it

  • MA100 as a trailing average of the matching block type over 100 heights, with its sample count, and a gap where the window is not complete.
  • The staked share over 100 and 1,000 blocks, the signed interval of the selected block, and the count of negative intervals.
  • The fee pool scales, the detail mode, the exact amounts, and the separate reading of the current pool.
  • Carbide's statement that the fee pool changes do not give the inflow or the payout.

What not to do

  • Horizon charts. Heer, Kong and Agrawala found that they save height but cost accuracy when the chart is not very small. Five short panels fit without them.
  • A learned anomaly band. A band from a model needs weeks of history and cannot be explained in one sentence. Process limits can.
  • A centred average as the main line. It cannot reach the present, and it is not Carbide's definition. It is useful only to show the delay.

One day of data is enough to see these shapes. The limits in the app must come from the range that the person loads, with a minimum number of points, as the process control guides require: 15 to 20 points for stable limits.
