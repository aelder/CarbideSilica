Chain Pulse Research

How the leading fields draw events in time, and what that means for the mined and staked block timeline. Each method below is drawn with the same hour of real Verus blocks, read from the local node.

The short answer: the research changes the design in one large way and two small ones. The large one comes from the data, not from the literature. Verus blocks do not arrive like a steady pulse or like Bitcoin's random arrivals. They arrive in bursts. The best designs make the burst the unit that the eye reads, keep each block visible inside it, and compare the chain with its own recent history in place of a fixed rule.

What the data is like

The usual model for block arrivals is the exponential distribution: blocks arrive at random, at a constant average rate. The chain pulse in the app today assumes that model. Its 5-minute and 10-minute thresholds come from it. The last 90 blocks do not fit it.

Two things follow. First, a picture with one mark at each timestamp puts several blocks on almost the same point. That is why the first design hid blocks behind each other, and why the rebuilt one must stack them. Second, a long gap is not rare here. In this sample, gaps of more than 5 minutes are several times more frequent than the random model predicts. A threshold from that model marks ordinary gaps as unusual.

The positions are block-header timestamps, not arrival times. The app cannot know when a past block arrived at the node. It can record the arrival time of each block that it sees while it runs, and the cache already stores an observation time.

What the leading fields do

Perception research

Cleveland and McGill measured which visual encodings people read most accurately. Mackinlay and Munzner turned the results into a ranking. Position on a common scale is first. Length is next. Area is poor. For categories, a separate region of space is the strongest channel, then colour hue, then shape.

For the pulse: put staked and mined in separate lanes and separate hues. Do not make size carry important data.

Neuroscience

Nerve impulses are the oldest well-studied case of events in time. The standard figure has two parts on one time axis: a raster with one tick for each event, and below it a histogram of the count in each time bin. The raster keeps each event. The histogram gives the rate. The choice of bin width is treated as part of the method.

For the pulse: show the events and the rate together. One of them alone is half the answer.

Monitoring and status pages

Grafana's status history and the uptime bars on public status pages divide time into equal cells and colour each cell by its state. They do not merge neighbours. A person reads health in one look, and a hover gives the detail. Brendan Gregg's latency heat maps apply the same idea to timing data, because an average hides the shape and a scatter plot overplots.

For the pulse: equal cells cannot overplot. They trade the exact time for a picture that is never wrong about the count.

Block explorers

mempool.space draws blocks as equal tiles in sequence, not at their true spacing, with the time written on each tile. beaconcha.in draws Ethereum's fixed slots as a grid and gives each cell two encodings: the border for the network state and the fill for your own validator. When a Bitcoin explorer showed a timer against the 10-minute target, Pieter Wuille objected that the time between blocks is memoryless, so the timer implies a schedule that does not exist. The builder's answer was that the display means "on time or late", not a prediction.

For the pulse: a sequence scale keeps each block legible. A target drawn as a rhythm claims too much.

Timeline research

Brehmer and colleagues surveyed 263 timelines and described the choices of scale: chronological, relative, logarithmic, sequential and collapsed. A chronological scale is not the only correct one. When events cluster, it gives most of the space to empty time and too little to the events.

For the pulse: a scale that compresses long gaps and labels them is a known, accepted form.

Statistical process control

Quality engineers chart the time between events on a t-chart. The limits come from the distribution that the process has when it is healthy. A point is marked only when it is outside those limits. Reliability engineers plot the cumulative count of events against time, where a steady process is a straight line.

For the pulse: take "unusual" from the chain's own history. Show the count against the target as a line that can be ahead or behind.

Uncertainty research

Kay, Hullman and colleagues tested how to show when a bus will come. People decided better with a quantile dot plot, where each dot is an equal share of the probability and a person counts dots. The same form shows where one value sits among many.

For the pulse: show the current wait among recent waits. "Longer than 9 in 10" is clearer than a red line.

Dashboard design

Stephen Few's bullet graph replaces a gauge with one bar for the measure, one mark for the target and shaded bands for the normal range. It answers "how are we against the target" in a small space.

For the pulse: the summary line can be one bullet: blocks in the window against the target, with the normal band.

The same hour, seven ways

Each figure uses the same data: the 60 minutes before block . Purple is staked. Amber is mined. The right edge is the newest block.

A. Each block at its timestamp

the app today, after the rebuild
Good: each block is visible, and the two kinds are apart.
Weak: stacks lean because each block keeps its own second. The 60-second ticks imply a rhythm. The gap labels use the random model.

B. Raster and rate

neuroscience
Good: the rate is read from the bars against the target line. The raster shows the texture.
Weak: ticks in a burst merge into one thick tick, so a block cannot be counted or clicked. The two parts need more height.

C. Minute cells

Grafana status history, status pages
Good: nothing can overlap. An empty stretch is as visible as a full one. It is very compact.
Weak: a cell is a count, not a block, so a block cannot be opened from it. The exact time inside the minute is lost.

D. Blocks in sequence

mempool.space, timeline research
Good: each block is the same size and can carry its height. The order inside a burst is clear. Long gaps are compressed and written.
Weak: distance is no longer time. Two strips cannot be compared by eye, and "how long ago" must be read from labels.

E. Bursts on a time axis

the unit that the data has
Good: each burst is one tidy column at one time. Staked blocks go up and mined blocks go down, one cell for each block. The height of a column is the size of the burst.
Weak: blocks a few seconds apart share one position, so the hover line must give the exact second.

F. Count against the target

reliability engineering
Good: it answers "ahead or behind" directly, and the band shows what is normal. Flat steps are gaps. Tall steps are bursts.
Weak: it does not show staked and mined, and it does not invite a click on a block. It is a companion, not the timeline.

G. The wait among recent waits

uncertainty research
Good: it replaces a fixed threshold with the chain's own record. A person counts dots to see how unusual a wait is.
Weak: it needs more history than one hour to be stable. A day or a week of gaps is better.

No single form answers all the questions. The fields that do this best use a pair: one figure for the events and one for the judgement. The neuroscience raster with its histogram is that pair. So is a status page's bar with its percentage.

What to build

The recommendation is form E for the timeline, with form G beside it for the judgement, under a one-line summary.

  1. Make the burst the unit. Blocks within a few seconds of each other become one column at one time. Staked cells go up from the line and mined cells go down. Each cell is one block and opens that block.
  2. Remove the 60-second ticks. They show a rhythm that this chain does not have. Keep the 5-minute grid for reading time.
  3. Take "unusual" from the chain. Compare a gap with the gaps of the last day or week. Write "longer than 9 in 10 recent gaps" in place of a fixed 5-minute rule. Use red only beyond the longest gap of the period.
  4. Show the current wait among recent waits. A small dot plot at the right of the timeline, with a marker that moves as the wait grows.
  5. Make the summary one bullet. Blocks in the window, the target, and the normal band, in one bar.
  6. Record arrival times. For blocks that the app sees while it runs, keep the time of arrival and place those blocks by it. Mark blocks placed by header time as such.

Form F, the count against the target, fits the Charts page better than the dashboard. It is the clearest answer to "is the chain on schedule" over a day.

What the rebuilt pulse has wrong

  • Its thresholds of 5 and 10 minutes assume random arrivals. The data shows that assumption is false for Verus, so the labels appear too frequently.
  • Its 60-second ticks suggest that a block is due at each tick.
  • Its stacks follow each block's own second, so a burst is a leaning pile and not one clear column.

The sample here is 90 blocks. It is enough to see the bursts. It is not enough to set the limits. The app must compute them from a longer period, which the Charts page already loads.
